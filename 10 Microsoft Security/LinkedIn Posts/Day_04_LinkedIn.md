## 1. Why This Topic Matters

Technical credibility gets you the interview. Personal credibility gets you asked back.

Most LinkedIn content from security architects is about tools, frameworks, and findings. Very little of it is about the human side of delivery. This post stands out because it is honest about something most contractors do not talk about: the work before the work. It also tells a real story, which is what people share.

For recruiters and hiring managers, this post answers the question they always have but rarely ask: what is it actually like to have this person on site?

## 2. LinkedIn Post

*(Stuart's own words — final version)*

---

How do I start a new project or engagement? And the start is really key for a successful engagement.

I was recently chatting to one of my friends about how I work and what I do, and how I handle new projects, so here is my approach and outlook.

When I arrive at a new engagement, I am not the most knowledgeable person in the room, but why? The team who have been there for three years know things I do not, and the worst thing I could do is walk in pretending otherwise. Walking into any room, you need to have an open mind.

So I start by listening. Not performing listening; actually listening. I want to know the names, the pressures, what people are worried about and what they are proud of. I am genuinely interested in the people around me; that is not a technique I have learned, it is just who I am.

To show this, I worked on an endpoint security migration for a financial services client a few years ago. The project came loaded with tension before I had even started, with tight deadlines, strong expectations from the business, and a delivery team under pressure for some time. That kind of environment can go either way. You can manage it at arm's length, stay transactional, and get the work done. Or you can actually get to know the people you are working with.

I always choose the second. Not because it is the smarter strategy, but because I find it almost impossible to work any other way. I bring a fairly cheerful character to most situations.

The real value of building those relationships became clear when, as it almost always does on a complex migration, something went wrong. Not catastrophically, but enough to need handling quickly and honestly. Because the trust was already there, the conversation was straightforward. No defensiveness, no escalation, no loss of confidence in the project. We talked about what had happened, agreed what we were going to do, and got on with it.

When you have built genuine trust with the people around you, being honest when something goes wrong is not nearly as hard as it might seem, and often is enlightening in the world we often live in.

What does your experience say? Is the relationship side of delivery underrated, or does everyone already know this and just not talk about it?

## 5. Follow-up Comment

Post within 30 minutes:

---

I should add: none of this means the technical work matters less. It absolutely matters, and getting that wrong is not something good relationships can fix for long.

But in my experience, the technical delivery goes better when the human foundation is in place. People tell you things earlier; problems surface sooner; solutions get agreed faster. It is not soft skills versus hard skills. The two work together.
