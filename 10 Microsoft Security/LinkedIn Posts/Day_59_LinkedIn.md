## LinkedIn Post (published — 2026-08-02)

Rest is where progress happens.

Yesterday I wrote about positive mental attitude (https://lnkd.in/p/ex7nxNjY) and some of the lessons I've learnt along the way. Today I want to share another lesson that sits alongside it.

For years, I thought progress came from doing more. My younger self was obsessed with that.

Ironman training taught me that progress comes from recovering from what you've already done.

When I first started training seriously, I believed that if everyone else was out training, I should be too. I found myself in an unspoken race to do more. Missing a session felt like I was falling behind and needed to catch up.

The result?

I spent far too much time injured and sitting in a physio's treatment room. I was training hard, ignoring good advice and not giving my body the time it needed to recover.

Instead of becoming stronger, I was simply becoming more tired.

And eventually, broken.

Everything changed when I started working with a coach.

Recovery weeks became part of the training plan, not an interruption to it. The sessions were shorter, the intensity dropped, and my body finally had time to adapt to the work I had already done.

I still train in build weeks, where the training load increases, followed by recovery weeks that allow my body and mind to adapt to the training completed.

Those easier weeks also gave me something else back.
* Time.
* Time with my family.
* Time to switch off.
* Time to enjoy life outside training.

The biggest surprise was that I didn't become slower.

I became more consistent.

Over time, I realised exactly the same lesson applies at work.

As a Microsoft Security Architect, there are times when I can spend hours trying to solve a design challenge and looking at the screen. Going backwards and forwards. Trying to find the right answer.

The harder I push, the less progress I sometimes make.

At that point, I have a choice.

Keep pushing or step away.

Today, I choose to step away.
* Go for a walk.
* Work on something completely different.

More often than not, the answer appears when I'm no longer trying to force it.

Scouting has become another way of recharging.

Working with young people and volunteers gives me a completely different focus. For a few hours, my attention is somewhere else. When I return to work, I often see the problem from a different angle.

I've learnt that recovery isn't about doing nothing.

It's about allowing your body and your mind to adapt, reset and prepare for what's next.

Athletes understand this.

More of us should apply the same thinking to our working lives.

Rest isn't the opposite of performance.

It's part of performance.

Sometimes, the most productive thing you can do is step away.

Have you found that stepping away from a problem has helped you solve it better than trying to push through?
