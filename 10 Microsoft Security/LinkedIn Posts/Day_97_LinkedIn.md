## LinkedIn Article (final — ready to post, 2026-09-09)

Lessons from nearly 100 days of posting

We are in the final few days of my 100-day posting challenge.

There have been wins, new connections and plenty of learning. Today I want to discuss what I got wrong.

Looking back through what I have written, the honest story needs a little more care than simply saying I kept going and it all worked out.

I underestimated the work involved.

By day 18, I was already writing that posting daily was more challenging than I had expected. By day 50, I had admitted there were long days when I was still writing at 9 pm to meet my daily goal.

That is the part you don't see when a finished article appears in your feed.

The research. The checking. Finding a way to explain something without making it harder to understand.

What helped was building a routine, planning the week on Sunday, and choosing a theme; four technical posts, Success Friday, and usually something more personal at the weekend.

By day 65, I wrote that limiting myself to four technical posts each week helped me keep the balance.

Consistency needed a plan, and the plan needed to fit around everything else in my life.

The response gave me something else to think about.

I wrote that some technical posts were generating great conversations. The personal ones usually received less interaction. And I was happy to keep writing them. On day 10, I shared my Ironman Lanzarote finish and the daily habits behind it. On day 17, I wrote about dancing through Water Orton with our Beavers, Cubs and Scouts at the carnival.

Those posts tell you something about me that a technical article cannot.

How I think. What matters to me. The people and experiences that influence how I work.

As I wrote at the time, I'm not Microsoft Learn, or an AI agent. I can add my own experience and perspective. Fewer interactions do not make that part of the story less important to me.

I underestimated how long building a presence would take.

On day 92, I wrote that it took more than 50 days before I started seeing real traction in the community I was trying to reach. That was my experience. That doesn't mean nothing happened before then.

At day 21, I was already reflecting on the response to the security articles. By day 30, I had accepted a job offer. On day 40, I shared that I was joining CyberOne. Those were things worth celebrating.

Building a presence in that community took longer. Looking back, I am glad I kept going beyond those early wins.

There were still difficult days.

Day 72 is a good example.

I sat down and wondered what I was going to write about. Did I have any ideas?

That day, I didn't.

So that became the post.

I wrote about how easy it would be to leave the kitchen table, take my coffee and do something else. Instead, I stayed until it was done.

By day 79, I was writing about the middle of the challenge. The excitement of the earlier milestones had gone, and the finish still felt some way off. I compared it to an Ironman. The work continues after the excitement has faded.

Telling people what I intended to do helped. I had made the commitment public. On those days, that mattered.

Another lesson was to pay attention to the questions people asked.

On day 62, I wrote about Defender for Containers. I spend little time on that subject in my day-to-day roles. Recent questions helped shape the article, so I explained container images, registries and Kubernetes before moving into the security controls.

Explaining the foundations helped me too.

By day 64, people were messaging me about careers, learning, confidence and decisions. I wrote about asking questions to help them think through their own situation.

Those conversations are part of the value of doing this.

And sometimes I needed someone else to help me think.

On day 85, I wrote about getting stuck on a task with a deadline I couldn't move. Asking for a review started clearing my thinking before the feedback had even arrived.

I became so engrossed that I got on the wrong train home from London. My son collected me and drove me back to the station where I had left my car.

Not my finest travel planning. But a useful reminder that I do not have to solve every problem on my own.

What would I keep?

The routine. The mix of technical and personal writing. The conversations. Being willing to say I don't know and then go and learn.

And the care that goes into what I publish.

Writing regularly has helped me improve. So have reviewing, rewriting and checking. I would not put speed above accuracy, particularly when people may use what I write in their work.

Nearly 100 days have given me plenty to reflect on. The wins were real. So were the late evenings, the blank pages and the need to ask for help.

I am glad I kept going. I am also glad I wrote about those parts while they were happening.

What have you got wrong during a challenge that taught you something worth keeping?
