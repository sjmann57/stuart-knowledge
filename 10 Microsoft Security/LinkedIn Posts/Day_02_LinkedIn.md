Zero Trust, the implementation reality vs the vendor pitch

Zero Trust has become one of those terms that means everything and nothing at the same time.

Ask a vendor what it is, and they will show you a product. Ask a consultant, and they will show you a framework. Ask a client who bought it, and they will probably show you a licence they have not configured yet.

The reality is that Zero Trust is not something you purchase, nor is it a project with a start and end date. It is a shift in how you think about access and trust across your entire environment, starting from the premise that nothing and nobody should be trusted by default, regardless of where they connect from.

In practice, that sounds straightforward. In a real organisation with legacy systems, hybrid infrastructure, and end users who want to get their job done, it is considerably more complicated.

I often talk with organisations, and they tell me MFA is enabled; that is all we need to do. Well, MFA is a good start, and I am not dismissing it, but it is a long way from the full picture. Identity is one pillar. You also need to think about device health, application access, data classification, and network segmentation.

Switching on MFA for Microsoft 365 sign-in while leaving everything else wide open is not a Zero Trust architecture, no matter what you may have been told.

Often, I have started by completing an end-to-end audit for a client, looking across the technical stack they have enabled and the products they have deployed. Often it looks good from the outside. Microsoft Secure Score says they are better than similar organisations, so they must be in a good place. The reality is that the organisation has often missed key strategic pillars needed to secure its environment due to a potentially insecure enablement of its security tooling.

The Microsoft stack gives you genuinely powerful tools to move in the right direction: Conditional Access, Entra ID, Intune, Defender for Endpoint. The tools are not the problem. The gap is almost always in how they are configured, and whether the organisation understood what it was trying to protect before it started deploying policies.

The most useful conversation I have with a new client is not about what tools they have. It is about what they are actually trying to prevent, their fears, and what they cannot afford to have happen. Starting there, and the architecture and solutions follow naturally. Start with the tools, and you end up with policies that do not map to the actual risk.

Zero Trust in your organisation: a genuine architecture, or a line on the security slide deck?
