## LinkedIn Article (Stuart's version — published)

Looking for a New Job? Here's What Helped Me Most

Most job searches start the same way: an unexpected change, a decision outside your control, or a conversation that does not go the way you planned. Here is what I did, and what actually helped.

What did I do and how did I approach my recent job search?

When I unexpectedly found myself looking for a new role, it made me question far more than my career. The circumstances were outside my control, but it still made me question my confidence, my value and how I presented myself professionally. Looking back, it also taught me a lot about finding the next opportunity. These are the steps I took:

CV

Let's start with what I did first. I looked at my CV and realised I needed to say more about what I had achieved, the organisations I'd worked with and the outcomes I'd delivered. I also needed to align my personal statement more closely with that philosophy. I had a great initial conversation with a Chief Operating Officer about a role, and he complimented me on my CV and its presentation after the changes. I also shortened it and streamlined it to focus on the past 10 to 15 years of my career, which is the most relevant. There are some great people talking about CVs and what they should look like; take some time to research and find your style.

LinkedIn

Next, with my CV in good shape, I updated my LinkedIn profile and job descriptions again to talk about what I have achieved. I updated my profile picture and banner, reviewed every section, added missing skills and rewrote my experience to focus on achievements rather than responsibilities.

Upgrading to the Pro version of LinkedIn was an investment; it gave me more information and helped me make new connections. The saying, "Your network is your net worth" always rings in my ears.

Your LinkedIn profile is no longer an online CV. It's often the first impression a hiring manager has before deciding whether to speak to you.

Get your CV & LinkedIn reviewed.

CV has been updated, and my LinkedIn review and tune-up are done; now is the time to put this in front of professional CV reviewers. Many will give you feedback for free, and if it really needs more attention, their services are a good option. My brother-in-law used that option to good effect when he was in a similar situation last year. I also shared my CV with some of my peers to get their feedback and make sure it accurately reflected who I am and what I do.

Job boards

Job boards were my next step, and I must admit they drive me a little crazy. Some adverts wanted more years of experience than the technology had even existed. Do not overthink this part; find a type of role that you would like to do, and think wider than what you have done in the past. I applied for roles I wouldn't normally consider, and that was very refreshing. I found my skills would fit very well. When you apply, always follow up, connect with the recruiter or hiring manager and be interested in the people and company, not just the role.

The job boards I found most useful were LinkedIn, Indeed & TotalJobs; I didn't really get on with CVLibrary, but I know others do. Do not just look at the first page; dig deeper; these sites also have an algorithm they use.

I also used LinkedIn to find and research the companies and people that I was talking to. I always researched the companies and the people I was meeting. It helped me ask better questions and remember that they'll almost certainly look at your online presence too.

Recruiters

We live in a world where we often hide behind a screen and a keyboard; this is not that time in my opinion. Wherever possible, speak to recruiters and contacts in your own network to get introductions. This proved very productive in my experience, and with a strong LinkedIn profile and CV, I found myself attracting people who wanted to talk with me.

I strongly recommend speaking with recruiters, whether internal or external to the organisation you are applying to, as building relationships has always been important to me. I have always, where possible, taken the time to get to know them, ask them questions, and find out about the role and the people. I have built strong working relationships with the recruiters I have worked with and would highly recommend a good few of them.

One of my recruiters is taking part in an Ironman, and I shared some of my own experiences with them, and we have kept in touch.

Interview Preparation

Make sure you have looked at who is interviewing you, the company, and know what you want to find out. For me, it has always been about the people and the culture. Have the questions ready for what you need to know about the role, people, and culture, and refine them at every opportunity.

After every interview, I made notes about the questions I was asked and the answers I could have improved. By the next interview, I was better prepared. Every interview became part of my preparation for the next one.

Do not stop; keep interviewing.

Going back to when I first started as a contractor in IT, I learnt a very valuable lesson: until you sign a contract, never stop applying and interviewing. Over the course of my search, I spoke to dozens of recruiters, interviewed with many organisations and eventually had multiple offers to choose from.

You have to maintain discipline and treat the process like any other project you would undertake.

Mindset

Many find job hunting burdensome, stressful, and difficult, but I look at it differently. For me, this is another day consulting: asking questions, listening to the problem, and offering options for resolving the issues, and hopefully I am the answer. Did I always see this positively? No, I, too, found it difficult, but as I have said in other articles, you need a cheerleader in your corner.

The other part is self-development: reading or listening to books like 'How To Win Friends, and Influence People', 'Who Moved My Cheese', 'The Magic of Thinking Big', 'Think and Grow Rich', and 'Feel the Fear and Do It Anyway'. Also, surround yourself with the people you want to become.

Looking back, I realised finding a new role wasn't about sending hundreds of applications. It was about improving something every day: my CV, my LinkedIn profile, my interview skills, my network, or my confidence.

Eventually, those small improvements led to conversations, interviews and offers. If you're looking for your next role today, keep going. One conversation can change everything.

Finally

One final lesson was not to take rejection personally. Sometimes another candidate simply had more relevant experience. Sometimes the role changed. Sometimes the company paused recruitment. Not every rejection reflects your ability, so learn what you can and move on to the next opportunity.
