## LinkedIn Post (Stuart's version — published 2026-07-27)

"Goals are Dreams with Dates On"

It's a saying I've heard many times over the years, and one that has stayed with me. Every January, social media fills with people sharing ambitious goals.

Run a marathon.
Get promoted.
Start a business.
Lose weight.
Write a book

A few weeks later, many of those goals quietly disappear.

I have set goals for most of my adult life. Some I have achieved. Some I have failed to reach. Others changed completely because life moved in a different direction.

Over the years I've found that success rarely comes from writing better goals. It comes from building better habits around them.

Know why the goal matters.

Before I commit to any goal, I ask one simple question.

Why do I want this?

If I cannot answer that honestly, I know my motivation probably won't last.

At different times, my goals have included:

* Becoming a better Microsoft Security Architect.
* Passing an exam.
* Completing sporting events, such as an Ironman triathlon.
* Helping more young people and adults through Scouting.

They are very different goals, but they all have something in common. They matter to me personally.

That makes getting up early, working late, or training in the rain a little easier.

Break big goals into small wins.

Getting ready for an Ironman is not a few weeks of exercise, but often a 12-month plan, from the physical training, food, sleep and mental preparation needed to get to the start line.

Large goals can feel overwhelming.

Instead of focusing on the destination, I focus on the next milestone.

When I wanted to improve my professional profile, I didn't set out thinking about recognition or awards. My goal was much simpler. I wanted to be seen.

So I challenged myself to publish one post on LinkedIn every day for 30 days.

I wasn't chasing awards or recognition. I wanted people to know who I was and what I could offer.

I focused on writing one article. Then another. After the first week, I had seven published. It became easier because it became part of my routine, much like brushing my teeth. I no longer had to think about doing it. It was simply what I did.

I don't think about writing every day. On Sunday, I plan the week. I choose a theme and decide what I'll write about.

Monday to Thursday are technical posts.
Friday is Success Friday.
Saturday and Sunday are usually more personal.

Training for an Ironman starts with short runs, swim drills and strength work. Nobody starts with a 20 km run after a 120 km bike ride. You build towards that over months.

Goals work the same way.

Write your goals down.

My goals live in more than my head.

I write them down.
I review them regularly.
I adjust them when circumstances change.

A goal that sits in a notebook for twelve months is unlikely to achieve much.

A goal you see every week stays in your thinking.

Measure progress

If you cannot measure progress, it's difficult to know if you're moving forward.

That doesn't mean every goal needs complicated spreadsheets.

Sometimes the simplest measures are the best.

* How many articles have I published?
* How many training sessions did I complete this week?
* How many customer conversations did I have?
* How many clients have I helped?

Progress creates motivation.

Accept that plans change.

One lesson I've learned is that changing a goal is not the same as giving up.

Sometimes new opportunities appear.
Sometimes your priorities change.
Sometimes you realise the original goal wasn't the right one; that's normal.

The destination may change, but the habit of moving forward should not.

Build systems, not motivation.

Motivation comes and goes. Some days you'll have plenty of it. Other days you'll have none. Systems are what carry you through on those days.

* I remove as many barriers as possible.
* If I want to exercise, I have my kit ready the night before a morning session.
* If I want to write, I already have a list of ideas.
* If I want to study Microsoft security, I block time in my diary.

Small systems reduce the number of decisions you need to make.

Celebrate progress

Learning to celebrate success has taken me time. Quite often it's something simple, like going out for a meal with Jeanie.

Always take a moment to celebrate; even in my working day, sometimes I turn the music up loud and dance round the house in celebration of solving a difficult problem.

For my last Ironman, we extended our holiday so we could relax and celebrate the achievement. An Ironman isn't a solo achievement. Family, friends and supporters all help you reach the finish line. Celebrating together felt just as important as crossing it.

Learn from people who have studied goals.

Many authors have influenced how I think about goals and habits, and I have talked about some of the books that have influenced me before.

Can't Hurt Me by David Goggins reminded me that we are usually capable of far more than we believe.

Run Britain by Nick Butter showed what can happen when an enormous goal is broken into thousands of manageable steps.

The Magic of Thinking Big by David J. Schwartz encouraged me to stop limiting my own ambitions before I'd even started.

Those books didn't give me my goals, but they helped shape how I think about achieving them.

My own approach

My process today is simple.

* Spend some quiet time thinking about where I want to be.
* Talk it through with the people around me.
* Picture what success looks like as though I've already achieved it.
* Write the goal down with a target date.
* Work backwards to identify the milestones.
* Break those milestones into monthly and weekly actions.
* Keep the plan somewhere I'll see it regularly.
* Review it, adjust it and keep moving forward.

Looking back, I've rarely achieved a goal exactly as I first imagined it. The destination has sometimes changed, and so has the route. What hasn't changed is having a direction to move towards.

Don't worry about making your goals perfect. Pick a direction that matters to you, give it a date, take the first step, review your progress regularly and keep moving forward. There will be setbacks, changes and moments of doubt, but they are all part of the process.

You may not end up exactly where you first planned, but if you stay consistent, you'll almost certainly end up somewhere better than if you had never started.

What goal are you working towards at the moment, and what small action will you take today to move one step closer?
