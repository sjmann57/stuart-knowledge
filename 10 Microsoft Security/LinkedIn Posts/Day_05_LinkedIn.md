## 1. Why This Topic Matters

Most candidates present themselves as available for anything. Stuart is not most candidates. A post that honestly describes what a good engagement looks like, and what the red flags are, does something most job search content never does: it makes the right people lean in and the wrong ones self-select out.

It also signals commercial maturity. Only someone who has done this long enough to know the difference between a good engagement and a damaging one writes a post like this.

For recruiters reading it, it is a clear brief. For hiring managers, it is reassurance that Stuart thinks about fit in both directions.

## 2. LinkedIn Post Draft

*(Written closer to Stuart's voice based on Days 1, 2 and 4 rewrites. Should need less editing than earlier drafts.)*

---

What do I look for in a contract opportunity? And equally, what makes me step back?

I have been contracting for a long time, and over the years I have learned that taking the wrong engagement costs more than not taking it. Not just financially; in time, energy, and the kind of work you end up with your name attached to.

So here is what I genuinely look for.

I want to work with an organisation that is serious about getting security right, not just getting a box ticked. There is a real difference between a client who wants meaningful change and one who wants a report to show an auditor. I can usually tell which one it is within the first conversation.

I want an honest scope. "We need a Microsoft Security Architect" covers a lot of ground. I am comfortable with complex and evolving briefs; what I find harder is discovering three weeks in that the actual requirement is quite different from the one described at the start.

I want access to the right people. Security architecture that does not reach the people who make decisions is just paper.

I work outside IR35. That is not a preference; it is how I structure my business, and it matters for how an engagement is set up from the beginning.

The red flags? A client who has bought every tool and wants someone to make them work without addressing the underlying gaps. A scope so vague that nobody involved could agree on what done looks like. And the one I have learned to spot quickly: an engagement where the pressure is urgent but the brief is thin. Urgency without clarity usually means the problem is not fully understood yet, and that is a much harder conversation to have once you have started.

I am not saying any of this to be difficult. The engagements that go well for the client go well because the foundations were right from the start. Honest conversations early save harder ones later.

What do you look for when assessing a new opportunity? And if you are on the other side, what do you wish contractors were more upfront about?

## 4. Engagement Strategy

**This post targets three distinct audiences simultaneously:**

Recruiters will bookmark it as a brief. If you have been messaging recruiters this week, some of them will already be watching your profile. This gives them the information they need to send you the right roles.

Hiring managers and procurement leads will either recognise the description of a good engagement and think "that is us" or recognise a red flag and quietly move on. Both outcomes are useful.

Security leaders and other architects will engage with the honest framing. The red flags section is the kind of thing people share because it validates their own experience.

**Before you post:**
Look for posts from recruiters in the Microsoft or cyber security space and engage with one or two. You are building visibility with that audience before the post goes live.

**Direct outreach tie-in:**
After posting, message 3 to 5 recruiters directly: "Posted something today about what I look for in a contract engagement — if you have anything in the Microsoft Security architecture space at the moment, happy to have a quick conversation."
