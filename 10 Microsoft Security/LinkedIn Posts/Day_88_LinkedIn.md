## LinkedIn Post (published — 2026-08-31)

Some of the best leadership lessons I have learned did not come from work

Some of the best leadership lessons I have learned did not come from work.

They came from Scouting.

As you may have read over the past 80+ days, Scouting is a big part of my life.

I started when I was eight years old as a Cub. At the time, Cubs was the youngest section you could join in the UK.

From Cub to Scout, then Venture Scout, which was later replaced by Explorers, and eventually becoming an adult volunteer, Scouting has been part of my life for a long time.

But none of that would have happened without adults giving up their time.

Today, more than 400,000 young people and around 100,000 adult volunteers take part in UK Scouting each week.

Those volunteers make it happen.

They give up evenings.

Weekends.

Sometimes a week of their annual leave.

They complete training, learn new skills, organise activities, manage risk and take responsibility for other people's children.

And they do it voluntarily.

Over the years, I have had the pleasure of helping build and lead volunteer teams in two Scout Groups as a Lead Volunteer.

It has taught me a lot about leadership.

Because supporting and guiding a team of volunteers is different from managing a team at work.

Or is it?

That depends on how you lead and how you see the people in your team.

People choose to follow you

At work, there is an employment contract.

There is a salary.

There is an understanding that you have been employed to perform a role.

That isn't negative. It is simply part of employment.

With volunteers, the relationship is different.

Nobody has to turn up.

Nobody has to give up their evening.

Nobody has to spend a wet weekend camping in a field.

They choose to.

For me, that means leadership needs to be about empowering, guiding, mentoring and helping people see what they are capable of doing.

You cannot rely on a job title.

You need people to want to come with you.

You lead by example

Volunteers notice what you do far more than what you say.

If you want people to be organised, be organised.

If you want people to stay calm when the plan falls apart, and in Scouting the plan will eventually fall apart, you need to show them what calm looks like.

If there is a horrible job that nobody wants to do, sometimes you need to be the first person doing it.

You cannot set a standard for everyone else that you aren't willing to follow yourself.

That lesson has followed me straight into my professional life.

I will never give a job that I would not do myself.

Find out why people are there

Everyone volunteers for a different reason.

* Some want to give something back.
* Some want their own children to experience what they experienced.
* Some want friendship.
* Some want a challenge.
* Some arrive with no idea what Scouting really involves.

Finding out why someone is there is one of the most interesting parts of leading volunteers.

Because once you understand what motivates someone, you can help them find their place.

* I have watched quiet, shy people grow into confident leaders.
* I have watched people who knew almost nothing about Scouting become the person others turn to for help.
* I have watched people learn skills they probably never expected to learn.

There is something incredibly rewarding about watching that happen.

Recognition matters

You cannot motivate volunteers with a pay rise.

So other things become important.

A genuine thank you.

Noticing that someone has gone above and beyond.

Asking their opinion.

Trusting them with something new.

Allowing them to lead.

And sometimes simply telling someone:

You did a good job tonight.

It costs nothing.

But it matters.

And perhaps that lesson applies to paid teams more than we sometimes admit.

Develop people, don't burn them out

There is always someone who says yes.

And I know a little bit about being that person.

* They'll run the activity.
* They'll organise the camp.
* They'll help another Scouting section.
* They'll fix the problem.
* They'll take another job.
* As a leader, letting them continue saying yes can make your life easier.

Until it doesn't.

Part of leadership is recognising when someone needs support.

Sometimes your job is to stop your strongest people carrying everything and help someone else step forward.

That means mentoring people.

* Giving them opportunities.
* Allowing them to make mistakes.
* And sometimes resisting the temptation to take over because you could do it quicker yourself.

You develop people, or eventually you burn out the people you depend on.

The goal isn't you

This may be the biggest lesson Scouting has taught me about leadership.

* The goal isn't you.
* In Scouting, we are there for the young people.
* But there is another responsibility too.

The people who come after us.

* You should be building something that continues when you are not there.
* If everything stops because one person leaves, you haven't built a team.
* You've built a dependency.

That applies just as much in business.

A good leader shouldn't need to be involved in every decision.

Your success should include watching other people become capable of doing things without you.

Perhaps even doing them better than you.

What Scouting has taught me about work

I have carried these lessons into my professional life.

Technical knowledge has helped me build my career.

But much of what I know about people came from somewhere else.

* Leading volunteers.
* Standing in fields.
* Solving problems when plans changed.
* Helping someone try something for the first time.
* Watching someone grow in confidence.
* And working alongside people who didn't have to be there but turned up anyway.

Paid teams and volunteer teams might be more alike than we think.

People want to understand why something matters.

They want to feel trusted.

They want the opportunity to grow.

They want their contribution to be recognised.

And they want to know that the person leading them cares about them as a person, not simply about the task they need completed.

So on this Bank Holiday Monday, my reflection is simple.

Some of the best leadership lessons I have learned did not come from work.

They came from Scouting and from the people I have had the pleasure of volunteering alongside.

And I am still learning from them.

What is the best leadership lesson you have learned somewhere other than work?
