## 2. LinkedIn Post (Draft — for Stuart's review)

My Garmin knows more about me than most security tools know about their network.

Training for an Ironman generates an enormous amount of data. Heart rate, power output, pace, cadence, sleep quality, heart rate variability, training load, recovery score. Every swim, every ride, every run logged, analysed, and compared against yesterday, last week, last season.

The technology available to endurance athletes today is genuinely remarkable. A device on your wrist tracks hundreds of data points per session. Apps correlate your performance across months. Your coach can see exactly what you did, when you did it, and how your body responded.

And yet the single biggest challenge in training is not collecting the data. It is knowing what to do with it.

I have had sessions where every signal was telling me to rest. Recovery score in the red. HRV down. Sleep quality poor. And I went out and trained anyway, because I had a plan, and I was going to stick to it. Two days later I was overtired, underperforming, and wishing I had listened.

Sound familiar?

In security, organisations collect enormous amounts of telemetry. Logs from every endpoint. Sign-in events from thousands of users. Risk signals from Identity Protection. Alerts from Defender. And in many of the environments I work in, the dashboards are full, the data is flowing, and nobody is acting on what it is telling them.

The Garmin does not make you fit. Training does. But training without paying attention to what the data is telling you leads to overtraining, injury, and events you do not finish.

Security tools do not protect you. The decisions you make based on what they tell you do. But having the dashboards and ignoring the signals is not a security posture. It is a very expensive false sense of security.

The parallel that always sticks with me is the coach. A good training coach does not just set the programme and leave you to it. They look at the data, ask the questions you are avoiding, and tell you to rest when you think you should push.

A good security architect does the same thing.

The data is there. In both cases. The question is whether anyone is paying attention to it, and whether they are making the right call when it matters.

What does your security data tell you that you are not acting on?
